# Long-Term Capital Management (LTCM) — Model Risk Failure

---

## Executive Summary

Long-Term Capital Management (LTCM) represents one of the most prominent failures of quantitative risk modeling in modern financial history.

The fund relied on convergence trades supported by statistical models that assumed stable relationships across markets. While these models performed well under normal conditions, they failed catastrophically during periods of systemic stress.

---

## Core Problem

LTCM’s failure was driven by a combination of:

- **Extreme leverage applied to small-edge trades**
- **Assumptions of stable correlations and volatility**
- **Reliance on models calibrated to historical norms**

When market conditions deviated sharply in 1998, these assumptions simultaneously broke down.

---

## Analytical Approach

Using yield curve data (1997–1998), the analysis evaluates:

- Volatility regime changes using rolling and EWMA methods  
- Principal components of the yield curve (shift, twist, curvature)  
- Differences between base and stress environments  
- Value-at-Risk (VaR) estimates versus realized outcomes  

---

## Key Findings

- Volatility increased rapidly during the crisis, exceeding model expectations  
- Correlations across positions moved toward one, eliminating diversification benefits  
- VaR estimates significantly understated potential losses  
- Leverage amplified relatively small model errors into systemic exposure  

---

## Key Insight

> LTCM did not fail because models were incorrect in form,  
> but because they were applied under assumptions that did not hold in stress conditions.

---

## Implications for Risk Management

- Risk models must incorporate regime shifts, not just historical data  
- Correlation assumptions should be stress-tested explicitly  
- Leverage should be evaluated relative to model uncertainty  
- Scenario analysis is essential alongside statistical measures
